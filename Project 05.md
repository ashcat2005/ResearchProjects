Undergraduate Thesis Proposal

1. Title

Relativistic Surface Imaging, Apparent Radius Distortion, and Thermal Spectral Lensing of Neutron Stars

2. Scope

When a distant observer views a compact star whose radius $R$ is comparable to its Schwarzschild radius $R_s = 2GM / c^2$, strong spacetime curvature dramatically alters the star's apparent size, visible surface fraction, and integrated thermal spectrum. Unlike Newtonian optics—where parallel rays from a distant observer intercept a spherical surface of radius $R$ within a circular disk of identical radius—light rays passing near a neutron star bend inward toward the gravitational potential well. Consequently, a neutron star presents an enlarged apparent radiation radius $R_{\infty}$ to a distant observer:

$$R_{\infty} = b_{\text{max}} = R\left(1 - \frac{2GM}{Rc^2}\right)^{-1/2} = R(1 + z_{\text{surf}})$$

Moreover, because rays grazing the stellar limb originate from the far hemisphere, more than $50\%$ of the stellar surface is visible at any instant, compressing surface features near the apparent edge into a highly distorted relativistic ring. If a neutron star possesses an inhomogeneous surface temperature distribution (such as cool equatorial bands and warm magnetic poles generated by anisotropic crustal thermal conduction), gravitational lensing weights regions near the limb differently than in flat spacetime, biasing standard blackbody spectral fits used to infer neutron star radii.

This undergraduate thesis focuses on backward general relativistic ray tracing from a distant observer's virtual image plane $(X, Y)$ to the surface of a static, spherically symmetric compact star described by the exterior Schwarzschild metric:

$$ds^2 = -\left(1 - \frac{2GM}{rc^2}\right)c^2\,dt^2 + \left(1 - \frac{2GM}{rc^2}\right)^{-1}dr^2 + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)$$

The scope encompasses three progressive modeling stages:

1. Backward Null Geodesic Ray Tracing: Propagating photon trajectories backward from a pixelated observer screen parameterized by impact parameter $b = \sqrt{X^2 + Y^2}$ until they either strike the stellar surface ($r = R$) or escape to infinity, mapping screen coordinates $(X, Y)$ directly onto stellar colatitude and longitude $(\theta_s, \phi_s)$.

2. Apparent Size & Ultra-Compact Lensing Regimes: Quantifying apparent radius magnification $R_{\infty} / R$ across realistic neutron star compactnesses ($R > 3GM/c^2$) as well as theoretical ultra-compact objects inside the photon sphere ($2.25\,GM/c^2 < R < 3GM/c^2$), where $b_{\text{max}}$ saturates at the critical photon capture radius $b_{\text{crit}} = 3\sqrt{3}\,GM/c^2$ and multiple secondary images appear.

3. Relativistic Surface Mapping & Spectral Distortion: Rendering gravitationally lensed 2D surface coordinate grids and synthetic thermal images, and integrating redshifted Planck/atmospheric intensities across the image plane to quantify how surface temperature anisotropies distort observed X-ray spectra (e.g., modeling isolated cooling neutron stars such as RX J1856.5$-$3754).

Rapid rotational frame dragging (Kerr spacetime), magnetospheric Faraday rotation, and time-dependent accretion disk occultation lie outside the scope of this project.

3. Objectives

General Objective

To develop, validate, and apply a backward general relativistic ray-tracing engine in Python to reconstruct 2D lensed images of neutron star surfaces, quantify apparent radius distortion across compactness regimes, and determine how gravitational lensing of inhomogeneous surface temperature maps biases observational blackbody radius measurements.

Specific Objectives

1. Backward Ray-Tracing Engine Implementation: Formulate the second-order Schwarzschild null geodesic orbit equation $d^2u/d\phi^2 + u = 3(GM/c^2)u^2$ (with $u = 1/r$) and implement an adaptive Runge–Kutta integrator (RK45) in Python that maps an observer's 2D Cartesian image plane $(X, Y)$ onto stellar surface coordinates $(\theta_s, \phi_s)$ and local emission angles $\alpha$.

2. Apparent Radius & Photon-Sphere Analysis: Compute the apparent stellar radius $b_{\text{max}}(M, R)$ across canonical neutron star Equations of State (SLy4, APR4) and ultra-compact toy models, verifying exact agreement with the analytical formula $R_{\infty} = R(1 - 2GM/Rc^2)^{-1/2}$ for $R \ge 3GM/c^2$ and capturing the photon-sphere saturation $b_{\text{crit}} = 3\sqrt{3}\,GM/c^2$ for $R < 3GM/c^2$.

3. 2D Relativistic Surface Visualization: Generate high-resolution lensed visualizations of latitude–longitude grids, continental/checkerboard test patterns, and magnetic dipole polar caps, illustrating limb compression, visibility of the antipodal pole, and secondary image formation.

4. Thermal Spectral Synthesis & Radius Bias Quantification: Integrate gravitationally redshifted local blackbody spectra $I_{\nu,\text{obs}} = (1 + z)^{-3} B_{\nu_{\text{em}}}(T(\theta_s, \phi_s))$ pixel-by-pixel across the observer's image plane for dipolar and quadrupolar crustal temperature distributions, fitting single-temperature blackbodies to the synthetic spectra to quantify the systematic discrepancy between inferred spectroscopic radiation radius ($R_{\text{spec}}$) and true physical radius ($R$).

5. Observational Benchmarking on Isolated Neutron Stars: Compare synthetic multi-temperature lensed spectra against published X-ray/optical spectral properties of the nearby thermally emitting isolated neutron star RX J1856.5$-$3754 (the brightest of the "Magnificent Seven"), demonstrating why single-blackbody X-ray fits systematically underestimate true stellar radii.

4. Deliverable

At the conclusion of the 4-month research period, the student will deliver the following artifacts:

1. Open-Source Software Package (pyStarLens):

	⚬ A clean, modular, PEP-8 compliant Python package built on NumPy, SciPy, and Matplotlib.

	⚬ Core modules for backward Schwarzschild null-geodesic integration, vectorized radial impact-parameter lookup tables for rapid high-resolution image rendering, spherical surface map projectors, and redshifted spectral flux integrators.

	⚬ Automated unit tests verifying flat-spacetime Newtonian projection limits ($C \to 0$), analytical apparent radius formulas, and Liouville's theorem ($I_\nu / \nu^3 = \text{const}$), accompanied by reproducible Jupyter notebooks generating all thesis figures.

2. Scientific Visualization & Data Suite:

	⚬ High-resolution comparative image plates contrasting Newtonian vs. relativistic views ($C = 0.15, 0.25, 0.35$) of neutron star latitude–longitude grids and magnetic polar caps.

	⚬ Diagnostic curves of apparent radius ratio $R_{\infty}/R$ and visible surface fraction $f_{\text{vis}}$ as functions of compactness $C = GM / (Rc^2)$ across the photon-sphere transition ($C = 1/3$).

	⚬ Synthetic X-ray spectra and color–temperature distortion curves illustrating the spectroscopic radius underestimation bias ($R_{\text{spec}} < R_{\infty}$) for non-uniform surface temperature profiles.

3. Undergraduate Thesis Manuscript & Defense:

	⚬ A formal LaTeX-typeset undergraduate thesis (40–60 pages) detailing Schwarzschild geodesic optics, image-plane projection geometry, numerical integration methods, surface imaging simulations, and spectral astrophysics implications.

	⚬ A 20-minute oral defense presentation summarizing the theoretical background, ray-tracing algorithm, visual results, and scientific conclusions.

5. 4 Months Timeline

The project is organized across 16 weeks (4 months) into four structured monthly phases with concrete weekly milestones:

Month 1: Schwarzschild Geodesic Optics & Image-Plane Geometry

⚬ Week 1: Literature review of compact-star gravitational lensing and surface imaging (Pechenick, Ftaclas, & Cohen 1983; Nollert et al. 1989; Kraus 1998; Page 1995). Review conserved photon energy $E$, angular momentum $L$, and impact parameter $b = cL/E$.

⚬ Week 2: Derive the null geodesic orbit equation in Schwarzschild coordinates in terms of inverse radius $u(\phi) = 1/r(\phi)$ and establish the relationship between observer screen coordinates $(X, Y)$, impact parameter $b$, and asymptotic photon direction.

⚬ Week 3: Implement the backward ray-tracing ODE integrator in Python (scipy.integrate.solve_ivp), integrating photon paths inward from a distant observer screen ($r_{\text{obs}} \gg R_s$) with event detection at the stellar surface ($r = R$) and turning-point detection ($dr/d\phi = 0$).

⚬ Week 4: Validate the numerical ray tracer against the Newtonian flat-spacetime limit ($GM/Rc^2 \to 0$) and analytical elliptic-integral deflection formulas, confirming pixel-to-surface mapping accuracy to $< 10^{-7}\text{ rad}$.

Month 2: Apparent Radius Magnification & Relativistic Surface Maps

⚬ Week 5: Compute the critical impact parameter $b_{\text{max}}$ across compactness values $C \in [0.01, 0.44]$, verifying exact reproduction of $R_{\infty} = R(1 - 2C)^{-1/2}$ for $C \le 1/3$ and capturing photon-sphere capture ($b_{\text{crit}} = 3\sqrt{3}\,GM/c^2$) for ultra-compact stars ($1/3 < C < 4/9$).

⚬ Week 6: Exploit spherical symmetry to build a fast 1D radial interpolation lookup table $\psi(b)$ and emission angle $\alpha(b)$, accelerating full $1024 \times 1024$ pixel image-plane rendering to sub-second execution times.

⚬ Week 7: Implement 2D spherical texture mapping to render high-resolution lensed images of latitude–longitude coordinate grids at varying observer inclinations ($i = 0^\circ, 30^\circ, 60^\circ, 90^\circ$), quantifying visible surface area fractions exceeding $50\%$.

⚬ Week 8: Simulate ultra-compact configurations ($R = 2.6\,GM/c^2$) to visualize relativistic Einstein rings and inverted secondary images formed by photons completing half-orbits around the star before striking the surface.

Month 3: Anisotropic Temperature Maps & Spectral Distortion

⚬ Week 9: Implement radiative transfer across the image plane using Liouville's theorem ($I_{\nu,\text{obs}} = g^3 I_{\nu,\text{em}}$, where $g = (1 + z)^{-1} = \sqrt{1 - 2C}$), verifying exact conservation of bolometric flux $F = \sigma T_{\text{eff}}^4 (R_{\infty} / D)^2$ for an isothermal blackbody star.

⚬ Week 10: Implement physically motivated inhomogeneous surface temperature distributions $T(\theta_s, \phi_s)$ induced by crustal dipolar and toroidal magnetic fields (e.g., Page 1995; Geppert, Küker, & Page 2004).

⚬ Week 11: Integrate pixel-by-pixel redshifted Planck spectra across the observer screen to generate synthetic X-ray spectra ($0.1\text{--}2.0\text{ keV}$) at different viewing angles and magnetic inclinations.

⚬ Week 12: Fit single-temperature absorbed/unabsorbed blackbody models to the synthetic composite spectra to quantify the apparent spectroscopic radius bias ($R_{\text{spec}} / R_{\infty}$) caused by limb-compressed cool equatorial regions.

Month 4: Observational Benchmarking & Thesis Writing

⚬ Week 13: Benchmark synthetic multi-temperature lensed spectra against published observational parameters of the isolated neutron star RX J1856.5$-$3754 (X-ray blackbody temperature $kT_{\text{X}} \approx 63\text{ eV}$ vs. optical Rayleigh–Jeans excess), demonstrating how gravitational lensing reconciles apparent X-ray and optical radii.

⚬ Week 14: Generate publication-grade thesis figures (lensed coordinate grids, $R_{\infty}/R$ vs. $C$ curves, ultra-compact secondary image maps, and spectral fit residual plots). Draft Chapters 1–3 of the thesis (Introduction, Relativistic Optics Theory, Numerical Ray-Tracing Methodology).

⚬ Week 15: Draft Chapters 4–5 (Surface Imaging Results, Spectral Bias Analysis, Observational Implications, and Conclusions). Clean, document, and finalize the pyStarLens GitHub repository.

⚬ Week 16: Advisor review, feedback integration, LaTeX formatting polish, final thesis submission, and rehearsal of the 20-minute defense presentation.
